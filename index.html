The four percent floor holds for 47 years, not 50

Run the four percent rule as a plain annuity: a million dollars, forty thousand a year, three percent returns, and the draw goes up with the market. The account survives 46.9 years. The rule earns its reputation at a fifty-year horizon only after rounding, and the gap between 47 and 50 is exactly where the rule gets its cautious name.

The mechanics are one line. Every year the money grows by three percent, then the draw comes out. A closed form covers it: the survival time is minus the log of one minus the ratio of three percent of the portfolio to the withdrawal, divided by the log of 1.03. With a million dollars and forty thousand drawn, the ratio is 0.75, and the log math lands on 46.9 years. Nothing else moves that number.

Set the draw to thirty-five thousand and the same account runs 65.8 years. Five thousand a year buys eighteen years of runway, which is the whole trade in one comparison. And the floor without growth is duller still: a million over forty thousand is 25 years, so every year of return above zero is what stretches the 25 into the 47.

The draw is the clock. Everything else is weather.
